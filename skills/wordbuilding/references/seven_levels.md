# The Seven Levels: What to Look For and Examples

Each level includes the core definition, what it looks like in practice, and concrete examples. Approximate timestamps refer to the source breakdown ("The Seven Levels of Worldbuilding").

These levels are not sequential steps. They are layers of understanding: you might realize Level 5 fits your world naturally while leaving Level 4 unaddressed. That is normal.

## 1. Map (03:32–07:42)

**Definition.** The terrain must look like it obeys rules—either Earth's or your setting's explicit physics.

**What it looks like.** Rivers flow downhill and converge; mountain ranges act as natural barriers rather than random spikes. Cities sit at water, trade crossroads, or defensible terrain—never spaced out symmetrically for visual balance. Deserts, frozen wastes, and prevailing winds have causes. If you break Earth rules (floating continents, arcane storms), you know why and apply the replacement rule consistently.

**Examples.** *Westeros*: the North is cold and sparsely populated because cold regions do not sustain huge populations; the Riverlands sit between the Vale and Westerlands so rivers naturally gather there; ports sit in deep water. *Discworld*: flat on a giant turtle, yet faithful to its own physical premise rather than arbitrary.

**What the script flags.** Rivers cutting randomly across mountains, cities placed solely for map symmetry, deserts existing only because "every map needs a desert."

## 2. Rule of Cool (07:42–10:00)

**Definition.** The map makes basic sense, and you fill it with things that excite you: dragons, a fallen empire nobody fully understands, a whispering cursed forest.

**What it looks like.** The world feels broad and full of ideas. Its strength is immediate intrigue—it sets tone and gives the setting flavor, which is often what draws audiences in first. With strong characters and storytelling, an entire work can succeed right here.

**Where trouble starts.** Mistaking "cool" for depth. Adding more gods and ancient wars expands breadth without adding depth. Structural questions remain unanswered: how does magic affect daily life? When the empire collapsed, what replaced it and what happened to ordinary people? If an artifact is that powerful, why hasn't it broken everything?

**Why this is a natural phase.** At this stage you discover what you actually love building; everything above this level comes from asking what those cool things truly imply.

## 3. Surface Identity (10:00–15:07)

**Definition.** The setting stops being generic fantasy and becomes a distinct place: naming conventions, architecture, attire, emblems, and recognizable cultures.

**What it looks like.** Do names in this region favor harsh consonants? Does this kingdom wear heavy plate or cheap leather? Stone or timber? Does the empire stamp sun motifs everywhere? One glance at a helmet silhouette tells you who made it.

**Examples.** *Lord of the Rings*: Elvish names follow one phonetic pattern while the Rohirrim follow an Anglo-Saxon historical echo—no labels needed. *Warhammer 40K*: the Imperium repeats cathedrals, skulls, and heavy armor across weapons, banners, and architecture. *Avatar*: Water Tribe (Inuit/Arctic), Earth Kingdom (East Asia), Fire Nation (imperial industrial Japan)—each spreads one core inspiration into color, clothing, environment, and motive.

**Watch out for.** "Planet of Hats"—reducing an entire culture to one trait (warrior race, clever race, sneaky race) where everyone behaves identically. Aim for recognizable identity, not uniforms.

## 4. Functional Power (15:07–19:03)

**Definition.** The world stops just looking different and starts behaving logically. When major forces exist (magic, tech, deities), they reshape everything around them.

**What it looks like.** Instead of asking "is this cool?" you ask "what does this actually do?" If magic is common, what happens to manual labor? If dragons exist, how do armies fight? When power has rules and consequences, you cannot solve plot problems by fiat; major events feel like accumulated pressure rather than coincidence.

**Examples.** *Mistborn*: specific metals grant specific powers with clear limits, producing a magic-wielding elite, different assassination tactics, and altered city security. *Dune*: thinking machines are banned, producing Mentats, Navigators, feudal religion, and shield-based blade combat. *Attack on Titan*: giant walls, concentric cities, vertical-mobility gear, and political legitimacy rooted in survival.

## 5. Deep Culture and History (19:03–22:49)

**Definition.** The world stops looking weathered and feels genuinely ancient. The question shifts from "how does this work now?" to "how did it end up like this?"

**What it looks like.** Culture is not outfits and flags—it is what people believe, argue about, feel ashamed of, and refuse to drop even when irrational. Traditions born from practical survival now treated as sacred taboos. History is not "there was a war 500 years ago"—it is the ripple effect: borders still contested, ruins warping trade, a split religion, place names preserving old wounds.

**Examples.** *The Witcher*: prejudice against non-humans grows from war, economic rivalry, and fear, varying by region; peasants, nobles, and city folk disagree and act on emotion. *The Silmarillion*: events reshape geography and oaths echo across ages until ruins in *Lord of the Rings* feel like dead kingdoms, not props. *The Elder Scrolls*: conflicting racial memories and competing histories justify present politics.

**Keyword.** Continuity: one age flows into the next, and deeds accumulate.

## 6. Infrastructure and Institutions (22:49–26:37)

**Definition.** Banking, trade arteries, military logistics. Note from the source: "deeper than most people ever need to go; plenty of great stories never operate here."

**What it looks like.** How does a city of 200,000 eat? How fast do orders travel across a continent-wide empire? If Level 4 is what sits under the hood, Level 6 is the gears inside the engine. Most worlds imply these gears off-screen; building them lets the world run without a protagonist.

**Examples.** *The Iron Bank of Braavos*: no armies or dragons, controls capital, and backs rival claimants when rulers default. *Fallout Vaults*: closed ecosystems governed by rigid social engineering where breaking one system ripples everywhere. *Snowpiercer*: the train is civilization; class and survival map directly to its cars.

## 7. Generative World (26:37–end)

**Definition.** The world generates stories on its own.

**What it looks like.** Zoom into any province, port, or era and you find pre-existing tension waiting to become a story. You do not force plots onto empty space—you uncover plots created by the world's setup. You could follow a king, a merchant, or a thief, or jump 400 years backward and tell an equally rich story. The world keeps turning even if you stop writing.

## Scope of What the Script Measures

The script only counts answers to 36 diagnostic questions. The numeric scale (`yes`=2, `partial`=1, `no`=0; `>=80` strong, `<50` weak) is the tool's own comparison aid so two evaluations of the same world can be tracked over time—it is not from the source video. The qualitative test remains simple: *is your world built on top of cool ideas, or on top of rules?*

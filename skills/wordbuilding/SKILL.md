# Worldbuilding Skill (Game Development)

## When to Use This Skill

- Designing a new game setting from scratch (any genre: fantasy, sci-fi, post-apocalyptic, historical, hybrid).
- Expanding an existing setting with new regions, factions, quests, or history.
- Auditing a game world that feels "shallow," "generic," or "incoherent" and diagnosing why.
- Generating lore content (item descriptions, environmental narrative, NPC dialogue, codex entries) that players actually want to read.
- Deciding what NOT to build — the most valuable worldbuilding decision is scope control.

Golden rule of scope: **the world exists to generate gameplay and stories, not to be an encyclopedia.** Players care whether the world feels interesting while they experience it, not how much work went into it.

## Part 1 — Core Principles (Rules + Case References)

### Rule 1: Start from ONE "What if?" twist on a familiar world
A limitless premise ("ancient telekinetic space samurai in a galactic empire") paralyzes builders with freedom. A productive premise changes exactly one thing about a world people already know.
- **Why:** Familiarity implies constraints; constraints make brainstorming generative instead of paralyzing.
- **Case:** *Toy Story* ("what if toys were alive?"), *Inside Out* ("what if emotions were people in your head?"), *The Incredibles* ("what if superheroes were illegal?"). Pixar shipped 9 original worlds in 15 years using this. Counter-case: the *Star Wars* pitch — brilliant result, nearly impossible starting point.

### Rule 2: Ask what the characters DO — their jobs build the world
For any setting, ask: "What is the average inhabitant's Tuesday like, and why?" The world should emerge as a natural reaction to what people do every day, not as a sculpted museum.
- **Why:** Asking "what's their job?" forces thinking in familiar, functional terms and produces a chain of consequences for free.
- **Case:** *Inside Out* — emotions' job is controlling Riley → control panel → memory storage → the subconscious as a physical place. *Monsters, Inc.* — scaring is a job → screams harvested as energy → door network. *WALL-E* — his job is trash collection → skyscrapers of compacted garbage explain the whole world in one image.

### Rule 3: Geography obeys rules — Earth's or explicitly its own
Rivers flow downhill and converge (they never split downstream or cross mountains). Cities sit at ports, rivers, and crossroads. Deserts need a cause. Cold regions support fewer people.
- **Why:** If the foundation is broken, everything above it feels unstable. Players notice, even when they can't articulate why.
- **Case:** *Westeros* (A Song of Ice and Fire) — cold empty North, converging rivers in the Riverlands, eroded irregular coastlines. *Discworld* breaks every Earth rule, but commits to its own internal system, so it works. Rule-breaking is fine; *unexplained* rule-breaking is not.

### Rule 4: Build hub-and-spoke, never in parallel
Introduce one core idea (the hub) first, then derive everything else from it. Never present magic, politics, history, and geography as four unrelated feature lists.
- **Why:** Isolated facts are forgettable; interconnected facts reinforce each other and are cheap to remember.
- **Case:** Gemstone-swallowing magic (hub) → guilds control gemstone rights (society) → a rebellion over gem access (history) → guild HQs in floating cities (geography). Every element now explains the others.

### Rule 5: Make power FUNCTIONAL — it must reshape the world
If magic, technology, or divine power exists, it does not sit there as decoration. It restructures labor, warfare, transportation, class systems, and architecture.
- **Why:** Functional power prevents plot-cheating: characters solve problems *inside* the system, which makes solutions feel earned. It also makes the world feel like it would run without the protagonist.
- **Case:** *Mistborn* — metal-based magic with hard rules → an elite magic caste, different security, different city design. *Dune* — thinking machines banned → human "computers," blade combat (shields), feudal religion. *Attack on Titan* — man-eating Titans → walls, verticality gear, a military-run government.

### Rule 6: Use extremes for emphasis — but only on a few pillars
Extremes create emphasis, and emphasis defines a world's identity. Prefer **qualitative** extremes (radical ideologies, extreme cultural practices, total prohibitions) over **quantitative** ones (bigger numbers, more planets). Choose 2–3 aspects tied to the game's core themes and dial them to 11; leave everything else moderate.
- **Why:** If everything is extreme, nothing is. Emphasis only works relative to a baseline.
- **Case:** *1984* and *Brave New World* are memorable because their societies are qualitatively extreme. *Dune's* Fremen water rituals are extreme because the desert demanded it; the ban on thinking machines is extreme even though its cause (the Butlerian Jihad) is long past. Counter-case: much of *Warhammer 40K*'s extremity is quantitative scale — fun, but not what creates depth.

### Rule 7: Let the world rot — layered ruins are free storytelling
Build the present ON TOP of older strata. Ruins are stories (a change in circumstance made physical) and ruins are questions ("who built this, and where did they go?"). Stack multiple ages of wreckage; let current inhabitants misunderstand, scavenge, and misinterpret the relics.
- **Why:** Decomposition conveys deep history with zero exposition. Every overgrown wall implies a timeline. Misread relics (a peaceful doctrine twisted into a war banner) generate tragedy and quests.
- **Case:** *Elden Ring* — a dragon corpse festering on an older church; walking mausoleums carrying second layers of tombs. *Skyrim* — broken walls no one explains. *Fallout* / *Mad Max* — entire cultures built on scavenging. *Adventure Time* — multiple stacked apocalypses. Real Earth: Greek fire, Roman concrete, the Bronze Age collapse — lost tech is realistic.

### Rule 8: Every setting is post-apocalyptic — Height, Fall, Shadow
Give the world three beats: a **Height** (the great lost age), a **Fall** (the catastrophe that ended it), and a **Shadow** (a present threat that could repeat the fall). The Fall must explain two things: why wonders are found in ruins, and why the present feels smaller than the past.
- **Why:** This structure generates mystery, scarcity, stakes, and a villain arc in one move. The Shadow connects lore to the active plot: players aren't just excavating the past, they're preventing its repetition.
- **Case:** *Forgotten Realms* (it's in the name), *Eberron* (the Last War just ended), *Ravenloft*, *The Lord of the Rings* (Mordor-era echo of Morgoth; Andúril reforged from shards). Historical basis: the actual Middle Ages were a post-Roman collapse — medieval people lived among ruins they couldn't reproduce.

### Rule 9: Scarcity turns loot into archaeology
Magic items and advanced technology should have origins, makers, and histories. They are found in ruins, vaults, and dead hands — not bought off shelves.
- **Why:** An item with a story is a quest hook and a piece of worldbuilding; an item in a shop catalog is a stat block. Scarcity creates value and wonder.
- **Case:** A named blade with a maker's mark that glows "as the old stories say" tells players the past was bigger. Reforging heirlooms (Narsil → Andúril) is worldbuilding through gameplay.

### Rule 10: Culture is deep, contradictory, and never a monolith
Give each culture recognizable surface identity (naming sounds, architecture, colors, silhouettes) AND internal variation: dissenters, hypocrites, competing interpretations, people who violate the mold.
- **Why:** A "Planet of Hats" (the warrior race, the sneaky merchant race) is a starting point at best. Internal contradiction is where quests, tension, and realism live. Conflicting versions of history make a world feel genuinely ancient.
- **Case:** *The Witcher* — prejudice against non-humans varies by region and has economic causes; religions layer and contradict. *The Elder Scrolls* — different races remember the same events differently; ruins are repurposed by later eras. *Avatar: The Last Airbender* — each nation has one real-world cultural anchor expressed consistently across clothing, architecture, and color, without making every character identical.

### Rule 11: Filter all lore through subjective experience — never dump it
The audience earns lore by being invested; they are never invested by lore dumps. Reveal the world through what characters notice, fear, misunderstand, and argue about. Leave gaps — "glimpses of a large history in the background" (Tolkien) are a feature, not a bug. In games this means: environmental storytelling, item descriptions, unreliable NPC accounts, and conflicting in-world documents instead of an intro cinematic history lecture.
- **Why:** People don't become interested in information because they're interested in the world — they become interested in information because they're interested in the world.
- **Case:** *Elder Scrolls* games open in prison, not with an Elder Scroll lecture. Best "info dumps" are *invited* — delivered because the characters urgently need the information right now (soldiers explaining a battlefield they're standing in), not because the author needs to unload it.

### Rule 12: Treat the world as liquid, not solid
The world must be capable of change — political, technological, geological — and the game's events should visibly change it. Static "medieval stasis" worlds feel like dioramas.
- **Why:** Change is the basic unit of plot; a world that can't change can't produce endings, consequences, or replayable divergence.
- **Case:** *Siege of Trevelyan* (interactive novel) — player choices destroy or save the city, splinter religions, sour or deepen relationships. Joe Abercrombie's *Age of Madness* — a fantasy world dragged into an industrial age. Even small recontextualization works: Privet Drive becomes emotional in the last *Harry Potter* book because we know we're leaving it.

### Rule 13: Build institutions that outlive the protagonist
Banking, food supply, communication, military logistics — the boring machinery. You rarely simulate it, but you must know it, because it constrains what rulers and factions can do and creates non-combat conflict.
- **Why:** Institutions give the world long-term memory and incentives beyond whoever sits on the throne this season. They are also quest engines: debt, famine, blockades, succession.
- **Case:** The Iron Bank of Braavos — no armies, controls capital, backs the opposing claimant when a crown defaults. *Fallout* Vaults — engineered closed ecosystems whose design decisions shape societies decades later. *Snowpiercer* — the train IS civilization; every conflict traces to infrastructure (who controls food, energy, security).

### Rule 14: Ship toward a GENERATIVE world
The end state: a world so internally sound that it generates stories by itself. You can zoom into any province, any faction, any era and find pre-existing tension waiting to become a quest. Plot is uncovered, not manufactured.
- **Why:** For game development this is the economic payoff — a generative world keeps producing expansions, side quests, and spin-offs without reinventing canon each time.
- **Case:** *The Elder Scrolls* and *Elden Ring* inspire massive player archaeology because every ruin implies more beneath. **Control test:** "If I stopped writing today, would this world keep moving on its own?"

## Part 2 — The Worldbuilding Workflow (Step by Step)

Run these phases in order for a new setting. For an existing setting, run Phase 9 (the audit) first, then backfill.

**Phase 0 — Define the pillars.** Decide the 2–3 core themes the game is about (e.g., "scarcity and hope," "tradition vs. progress"). Every extreme, every faction, every ruin should serve these. If you can't name the themes, you can't decide what to emphasize later.

**Phase 1 — Premise.** Write the "What if?" as ONE twist on a familiar world. Test: could a 5-year-old AND a 45-year-old both immediately rattle off three ideas from it? If neither can, it's either too abstract or too limitless — tighten it.

**Phase 2 — Jobs.** Ask what the inhabitants DO. What is their average Tuesday, and why? Derive locations, tools, dangers, and social structures as consequences of those jobs. In games, also ask: what jobs does the *player* interact with (guards, merchants, couriers, cultists)?

**Phase 3 — Geography pass.** Sketch the map with cause and effect: rivers converge downhill; cities at water/trade/defense; mountain ranges create rain shadows; coastlines are irregular. If the world breaks Earth logic (floating continent), state the replacement rule and apply it consistently. This pass also feeds level design: chokepoints, trade routes, and natural dungeon sites.

**Phase 4 — Functional power pass.** For each power system (magic, tech, gods), trace the cascade: Who gets power? What happens to labor, warfare, travel, class structure, architecture? Write 1–2 hard rules and 1–2 costs per system. If a power has no cost or limit, it will destabilize both the world and the plot.

**Phase 5 — History layering.** Write the Height, the Fall, and the Shadow (Rule 8). Then stack 2–4 ruin strata: older civilizations beneath the recent one, each partially misread by the present. Decide what knowledge was LOST (this creates scarcity and mystery) and what survives only in distorted form.

**Phase 6 — Culture & factions.** For each faction: one real-world cultural anchor, a consistent surface identity (naming, silhouette, color, material), one internal contradiction (dissenters, reformers, hypocrites), and a stake in the Shadow. Faction conflict should be traceable to resources, history, or interpretation — not just "they are evil."

**Phase 7 — Institutions pass.** Answer briefly: How does the biggest city eat? How do orders travel from capital to frontier? Who lends money, and what happens on default? One paragraph per answer is enough — you're constraining the world, not simulating it.

**Phase 8 — Extremes pass.** Pick the 2–3 pillars aligned with Phase 0's themes and dial them up — qualitatively where possible. Derive their consequences into daily life. Everything else stays moderate on purpose.

**Phase 9 — Content generation audit.** Zoom into 5 random points (a port, a mine, a monastery, a border, a family). Does each contain pre-existing tension you could turn into a quest without inventing anything new? If not, the world is a backdrop, not a world — return to Phases 5–6 and add stakes.

**Phase 10 — Slice and ship.** Build only what the current game needs. Leave deliberate gaps (unvisited islands, unnamed ruins, unexplained gods). Write lore the player can *encounter*, not lore they must read. Never let the document exist to support the world when the world exists to support the game.

## Part 3 — The Seven Levels (Maturity Ladder)

Use as a diagnostic: identify the level of an existing world, then target the next level. Levels are tiers of understanding, not mandatory steps — great games ship at Level 2–4 all the time.

1. **Map** — The land obeys rules (rivers, cities, climates). Foundation logic.
2. **Rule of Cool** — Dragons, cursed forests, fallen empires. Grabs attention and creates tone. Valid, as long as you don't mistake it for depth.
3. **Surface Identity** — Naming conventions, architecture, silhouettes. Players can tell where they are without a title card. Danger: Planet of Hats.
4. **Functional Power** — Powers have rules and reshape society; the world would react this way whether the hero existed or not.
5. **Deep Culture & History** — The world has a memory: ripples of old wars, contradictory beliefs, unreliable histories, evolving traditions.
6. **Infrastructure & Institutions** — Banking, logistics, supply. The machinery runs without protagonists.
7. **Generative World** — Structurally sound; the world produces stories on demand at any zoom level.

### Deterministic 7-Level Profiler (`scripts/world_levels.py`)

Run commands from this skill's directory (`skills/wordbuilding/`):

| Situation | Command / Reference |
|---|---|
| Review detailed level definitions & video examples | Read `references/seven_levels.md` |
| Generate blank 36-question checklist (all levels or one) | `python scripts/world_levels.py checklist [--level 4]` |
| Score a completed world profile | `python scripts/world_levels.py evaluate world.json --json result.json --output profile.md` |
| Preview output on sample data ("Ash Coasts") | `python scripts/world_levels.py evaluate assets/sample_world.json` |

**Scoring & workflow rules:**
- Valid answers are `yes` (2), `partial` (1), `no` (0), and `na` (excluded—use `na` only when a question truly does not apply, e.g., continent maps in a single-city story).
- Four questions have `direction: "risk"` (inverted scoring: `yes` triggers a risk warning). Never hide these.
- A level is `strong` at `>= 80` with zero `0`-point items, `weak` at `< 50`, and `partial` in between. These numeric thresholds and 2/1/0 points are the profiler's own comparison scale, not rigid rules from the source analysis.
- **Open questions below your highest strong level** are the primary finding—patch lower-level foundations before pushing deeper, and only recommend Level 6 when a project actually needs institutional depth.
- Apply three diagnostic questions to any world element (city, guild, religion): *Why is it here? Who benefits from it? What happens if this changes?* If you cannot answer all three, score it `partial`.
- Verify claims against the actual world document/map before marking `yes`; if a document omits something, mark `no` or `na` with `note: "not in document"`.
- Run `python -m unittest tests/test_world_levels.py` after editing `scripts/world_levels.py`.

## Part 4 — Anti-Patterns: The Seven Deadly Sins (Game Edition)

Each sin is survivable in small doses; fatal when habitual. Include the fix in every review.

1. **Pride — The Lore Dump.** Explaining the world in creation-order (myth → gods → dynasties) because that's the order you invented it. Fix: does the audience need this *right now*? Lead with story, withhold with mystery; players read the codex because they already fell in love with the world, never the reverse.
2. **Greed — Franchise Before Story.** Merch, monetization, and sequel planning before the core experience is good. Fix: make something people care about first; the rest follows.
3. **Wrath — Edgelord Inflation.** Everything is torture and ruin; darkness as a competitive flex. Fixes itself wrong twice: shock loses its punch through repetition, and a world where "every village is raided monthly" cannot explain its own functioning cities. Fix: darkness hits only against a lighter baseline, and grim settings still need believable social logic.
4. **Envy — Cloning.** Europe rotated 20°; Middle-earth with extra islands; a metal-eating magic system in a world that isn't a legal distance from Mistborn. Test (per Sanderson): how common is the idea, and how big is the source? Common ideas are safe; signature ideas of a famous IP are not. Fix: transform, don't transplant.
5. **Lust — Rule of Cool Without Depth.** Cool ideas used as decorations instead of building blocks. A dragon isn't just a dragon — it changes food chains, warfare, insurance, and architecture. Fix: keep the cool thing; answer "what does this actually do to the world around it?" (Ironically this makes it cooler: a floating city with considered trade logistics beats a floating city.)
6. **Gluttony — Worldbuilder's Disease.** Every corner needs a history; hundreds of pages of lore for a game that hasn't started production. Fix: worldbuilding supports the project; the moment the project exists to support worldbuilding, stop. Build what gameplay will touch; leave the rest as glimpses.
7. **Sloth — Perpetual Restart.** Endless new settings, map drafts, and magic systems; a graveyard of half-finished worlds while nothing ships. Fix: pick one path and keep walking. Finished works are behind thousands of unpursued ideas, and that's fine.

## Part 5 — Control Questions (Audits)

Run the relevant audit before committing content. Any "no" points at the phase to revisit.

**Premise audit**
- Is it ONE twist on a familiar world (not a limitless cosmos)?
- Can both a newcomer and a veteran immediately generate ideas from it?

**Geography audit**
- Do rivers converge downhill? Are cities at water/trade/defense for stated reasons?
- If the world breaks Earth logic, is the replacement rule explicit and consistently applied?

**Power audit**
- Does every power system have hard rules, limits, and costs?
- Have you traced its effect on labor, war, class, travel, and architecture?
- Could the current plot be "solved" by a power that ignores the system? If yes, patch it.

**History audit**
- Do Height, Fall, and Shadow all exist — and does the Fall explain why wonders lie in ruins and why the present feels smaller?
- Are there at least two ruin strata, with the older one partially misunderstood by the present?
- Is there lost knowledge or lost technology that makes relics scarce and meaningful?

**Culture audit**
- Does each faction have surface identity (naming, silhouette, color) AND internal dissent?
- Is any culture a single trait in a hat? Add contradiction.
- Do different groups remember the same historical events differently?

**Player-experience audit**
- Is all delivered lore filtered through a viewpoint (what does THIS character/NPC/item notice and get wrong)?
- Are info dumps invited (urgently needed by characters in-scene) rather than scheduled?
- Does the audience earn the lore? Would cutting 30% of it make the world more mysterious rather than thinner?

**Generative audit**
- Pick 5 random locations: does each hold pre-existing tension that becomes a quest without new invention?
- If you stopped building today, would the world keep moving on its own?
- Can you name what the world is about (themes) and which 2–3 elements are deliberately extreme — and why nothing else is?

## Part 6 — Quick Reference Card

**Pipeline:** Theme pillars → "What if?" premise → Inhabitants' jobs → Geography rules → Power cascades → Height/Fall/Shadow → Culture with contradictions → Institutions → Extremes on 2–3 pillars → Generative audit → Ship a slice.

**Ten questions that fix most worlds:**
1. What is the one "what if" twist?
2. What do people here do all day, and why?
3. Why is this city where it is?
4. What does the magic/tech actually cost, and what did it change?
5. What great thing was lost, and who misremembers it?
6. What fell, what shadow remains, and who wants to repeat it?
7. Where does this culture disagree with itself?
8. Who lends the money, and who moves the food?
9. Which 2–3 things are extreme on purpose — and what stays moderate?
10. If I stopped writing now, would this world still generate stories?

**Remember:** Build the world to break, scar, and keep turning without you. Leave questions unanswered. Let players dig. Every relic is a story, every ruin is a question, and every answer they find should feel like survival — not homework.
